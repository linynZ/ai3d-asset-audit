\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the problems were.} We expected generated meshes to fail as
meshes. They did not: mesh topology caused no fix, and by our measures the raw
output is as closed, as manifold and as efficiently unwrapped as the meshes the
game bought. The problems sat around the mesh. The generator normalises size to
one metre whatever the subject. The importer reversed the front. The import path
stored each asset's texture uncompressed, mostly with a CPU copy, and dropped
two of its three maps. Our simplification tore the companion. An animation
lifted the boss off the ground. Of these, only the size normalisation and the
animation belong to the generator; the rest belong to the pipeline built around
it: a third-party importer and tools the assistant wrote.

\paragraph{Where the explanations went wrong.} The assistant was
mostly right when it read numbers off files and less reliable when it related an
asset to the pipeline. Four of its eight wrong claims named the newest and most
conspicuous component, the generator, as the cause of what an older one, the
importer or our own script, had done. One of them shows how such an explanation
lives on. Written as the comment on the line it justifies
(\texttt{\_faceYaw = 180f; // Hunyuan front-view exports face -Z}), it was cited
two weeks later in another tool as an ``NPC-pipeline finding'' and became an
expectation in a third tool's design note. Of the wrong claims that were
revised, one was revised after its symptom appeared on screen and the other only
in a later commit message, leaving the design note unchanged. That fixes can
carry new defects is well known~\cite{yin2011fixes}; here a fix also carried an
explanation that outlived it. A cheap safeguard follows: when an assistant
writes a cause into a comment or a design note, attach the check that would
confirm it, or mark it as a hypothesis.

\paragraph{Play-testing sees only what is on screen.} Every defect a player could
clearly see was fixed, apart from an idle bob that was reported and accepted.
Of the defects not visible on screen, all but one shipped: an uncompressed
texture costs memory, not pixels; a missing normal map looks like a stylistic
choice; a hairline crack on a small companion passes a play check. The exception,
a normal map imported with the wrong type, was caught by the assistant's
pre-release code review, a check that does not depend on looking. The
uncompressed textures had been flagged by an AI review and were not acted on,
with no reason recorded. Where the developer's main check is to run the game and
look, non-visual checks of this kind are what cover the gap.

\paragraph{A pre-import checklist.} Derived from this case and not validated on
other projects.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Size and front.} Treat the largest extent as unknown and supply real
  size from the design. Render one view from $+Z$ before import and one after, to
  separate the asset's front from the importer's axis convention.
\item \emph{What the engine actually stores.} Read texture format, size,
  readability and which maps the material references from the build, not from the
  source file; an uncompressed 4096$^2$ map is four times its BC7 size, and a
  dropped normal map is invisible to a memory check.
\item \emph{Simplification input.} Weld before simplifying and count boundary
  edges after; a seam-split buffer simplifies without any error message, and in
  MeshLab usually returns the requested triangle count.
\item \emph{Animated contact.} For rigged assets, evaluate every clip offline and
  compare its lowest vertex with the bind pose and with the clip used for
  placement.
\item \emph{Explanations.} Treat a cause written by the assistant as a hypothesis
  until a measurement backs it, and keep that measurement next to the comment.
\end{enumerate}
Items 1 and 3 restate existing guidance on real-world scale and
origin~\cite{khronos3dc2025guidelines} and on welding before
simplification~\cite{meshopt}, and texture memory is a familiar
concern~\cite{unity_mobileart}; what this case adds is where the violations came
from, which ones went unnoticed, and how their explanations went wrong.
